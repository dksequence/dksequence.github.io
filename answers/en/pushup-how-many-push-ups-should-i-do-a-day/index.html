How many push-ups should I do a day?

There is no official daily push-up number. The CDC and NHS call for muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days a week, and ACSM suggests 2 to 3 days a week for beginners. Pick a total you can finish with clean form, split into 3 to 5 sets, and rest between sessions rather than grinding every day.

Why this happens

Health agencies talk about strengthening days, not push-up counts. The CDC and the NHS both recommend muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week that works all the major muscle groups, and push-ups cover only part of that list: chest, shoulders and arms, plus some abdomen.

So the useful question is not what number is correct, but what number you can repeat with good form this week and slightly beat next week. That number depends on your starting level, and a short test will tell you.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Test your clean-rep maximum first

    After a short warm-up of arm circles and a few slow wall push-ups, do one set of standard push-ups with a straight line from head to heels and your chest going down to about a fist's height from the floor. Stop the moment your hips sag or your elbows flare wide. Write down that number. Everything else on this page is built on it, because a goal chosen without a baseline is a guess that either bores you or hurts you.

    Time: 3 minutes · You need: A floor and a pen or notes page

  2. Pick the variation that fits your number

    If you managed fewer than about 8 clean reps, switch to a version you can do for 8 to 12: hands on a wall, a counter or a sturdy table, or on your knees. The ACSM progression statement recommends that beginners use a load that matches an 8 to 12 repetition maximum, and in push-up terms that means choosing the angle where 8 to 12 reps feel hard but tidy. If you managed 12 or more, stay with the floor version. Doing 30 sloppy reps is worse than 10 good ones.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: A wall, counter or table if you need an incline

  3. Set a daily total at roughly three sets of your comfortable number

    Take about 70 percent of your clean maximum and do it for 3 sets. That is a deliberately easy starting target, so a person whose max is 10 starts with 3 sets of 7, about 21 a day on training days. For comparison, the NHS gives 3 sets of 5 to 10 repetitions as its wall press-up target, so a total in that range is a normal beginner dose rather than a small one. Round the total to something you can remember, and write it down before you start.

    Time: 1 minute to plan · You need: A calculator or your head

  4. Use rest between sets and rest days between sessions

    Rest 1 to 2 minutes between sets, which fits the ACSM range for muscle-building work, and train on 2 or 3 days a week, such as Monday, Wednesday and Friday. The ACSM recommends 2 to 3 days a week for novices, and the CDC and NHS set 2 days a week as the minimum. The NHS also says to work to the point where you need a short rest before repeating the activity, so you should feel the last rep of the last set, not crawl through it.

    Time: 10 to 15 minutes per session · You need: A clock or a phone timer

  5. Add a little each week, not each day

    When the full target feels easy for two sessions in a row, add 1 or 2 reps per set, or move to a lower incline. The ACSM progression guidance suggests increasing the load when you can do one to two repetitions more than the current target, and the NHS tells beginners to build up slowly and gradually increase the repetitions. Keep a simple log of date, sets and reps so you can see when that moment arrives. If a joint hurts, as opposed to a muscle simply tiring, stop and ask a clinician before continuing.

    Time: 1 minute per session to log · You need: A notebook or notes page

Where this approach stops working

  • No public-health body publishes a push-up quota, so any single daily number you read online is an opinion; the plan above only finds your own starting point.
  • Counting reps by yourself is surprisingly hard while your arms shake, and miscounts nearly always round up.
  • Timing rests with a clock means picking up your phone with sweaty hands, which tends to turn 90 seconds into five minutes.
  • A paper log only works if you remember to write it down, and a missed day quietly becomes a missed week.
  • Form drifts as you tire, and nobody is watching to tell you that the last five reps stopped counting.

Questions people also ask

Is it okay to do push-ups every day?

The guidelines set a minimum of 2 days a week of strengthening activity and do not set a ceiling; ACSM suggests 2 to 3 days a week for novices. Daily light sets are possible for some people, but if your chest, shoulders or elbows feel sore the next morning, take the next day off.

How many push-ups is considered good?

There is no official standard for the general public. One observational study of 1,104 male firefighters, published in JAMA Network Open in 2019, linked doing more than 40 push-ups to a lower rate of cardiovascular events than doing fewer than 10. It was an association in one group of men, so treat 40 as an interesting benchmark, not a target you must reach.

Can I do 100 push-ups a day to get stronger?

Nothing in the guidance above requires it. A beginner's dose is closer to 3 sets of 5 to 10, and adding reps slowly is the NHS advice. Chasing a big round number early is a common way to end up with sore elbows and quit.

Do push-ups replace other exercise?

No. The CDC pairs its strengthening advice with 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week for adults, and it names all major muscle groups, including legs, hips and back, which push-ups barely train.
