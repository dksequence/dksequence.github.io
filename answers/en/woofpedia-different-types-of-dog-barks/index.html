The different types of dog barks

Sort barks two ways at once. By situation, behaviourists name seven: territorial, alarm, attention-seeking, greeting, compulsive, socially facilitated and frustration-induced. By sound they are one graded range, from rough, low, unmodulated barks in fast volleys to clear, higher, modulated barks with gaps. Identify one by combining both: how it sounded, and what the dog was doing.

Why this happens

The seven-way list comes from the ASPCA's behaviour material, and each entry has a tell. Territorial barking is aimed at people or animals approaching a place the dog treats as his. Alarm barking goes off at any sound or sight anywhere, not just at home, and comes with a stiffer body and a step forward on each bark. Attention-seeking barking is aimed at you and pays off in food, toys or contact. Greeting barking arrives with a loose body and a wagging tail. Compulsive barking is repetitive, often with a repetitive movement to match. Socially facilitated barking only happens when other dogs are barking. Frustration-induced barking shows up when a dog is confined or cannot reach something it wants.

Acoustically, though, those seven do not come in seven flavours. Yin and McCowan analysed 4,672 barks from ten dogs of six breeds in 2004 and concluded that barks are graded vocalizations, ranging continuously from harsh, low-frequency, unmodulated calls to harmonically rich, higher-frequency, modulated ones. The doorbell situation clustered at the rough low end and was also longer with faster repetition; isolation and play clustered at the clear high end. The types are regions on one dial, not separate words.

Two practical consequences follow. A sound on its own is not a diagnosis, because isolation and play barks sit in the same acoustic neighbourhood and only the situation tells them apart. And the ranges are personal: the same study found individual dogs identifiable from their bark spectrograms regardless of context, which is why a fairly low bark from a small dog can still be that dog's warning.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Learn the three dials before you learn the labels

    Write them on a card: pitch (low, mid or high for this dog), harshness (rough and buzzy versus clear and almost musical), and spacing (a rapid volley versus single barks with gaps). Every type below is a combination of those three plus a situation. A review in the journal Dog Behavior sums up the direction of travel neatly - high tones call attention, low tones convey threat, duration relates to urgency and repetition rate to excitement.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: An index card on the fridge

  2. Mark which of the seven your dog actually does

    Go through the list - territorial, alarm, attention-seeking, greeting, compulsive, socially facilitated, frustration-induced - and tick only the ones you have genuinely seen. Most dogs do two to four, not seven. People skip this step, and skipping it is why barking feels like one undifferentiated problem: a dog that does greeting and frustration barking needs completely different handling from a dog that does territorial and alarm barking. Next to each type you tick, write the two or three triggers you have actually seen it with: the bin lorry, the doorbell, the lead coming off its hook, the neighbour's dog at six in the evening. A type with no trigger beside it is a label you borrowed from an article rather than something you watched happen.

    Time: 15 minutes · You need: Honesty about what you have actually observed

  3. Collect one clean recording of each type over a week

    Ten to twenty seconds each, phone at arm's length, and rename the file straight away with the type and the trigger: alarm_bin_lorry, greeting_grandma, frustration_lead_on. Building your own reference set is the whole trick, because the reason types feel confusing is that you are always comparing the bark you are hearing now against one you half-remember from last Tuesday. Try to catch the trigger in the same clip, even faintly. A recording that contains the doorbell and then the barking proves the pairing; a recording of barking on its own proves nothing to you a week later. Back the files up somewhere you will still have them next month, because the value of the set grows with its age.

    Time: A week, a few seconds at a time · You need: A phone recorder and a naming habit

  4. Blind-test your labels on somebody else

    Play three of your clips to a housemate with no context and ask them to pick the situation from your list. Pongracz and colleagues showed that untrained listeners categorise recorded barks well above chance, so if nobody in the house can tell your dog's alarm bark from his greeting bark, the labels are probably wrong and the recordings need redoing with better notes about what was happening at the time.

    Time: 10 minutes · You need: One willing person who has not seen your notes

  5. Listen to other dogs on purpose

    Spend twenty minutes at a park or outside a training class listening rather than watching. Body size shifts pitch, so the only way to know whether your dog's bark is genuinely low is to have heard a range of dogs. This also heads off the most common mistake in the whole exercise: reading a small dog's high, fast barking as friendly and a large dog's low barking as hostile, when both may be doing exactly the same job. One drill is worth the trip: shut your eyes for a minute and name what each dog is doing before you look. Vision overrides hearing so completely that most people have never really listened to a bark until the picture is taken away.

    Time: 20 minutes · You need: A park bench and some patience

Where this approach stops working

  • The categories overlap on purpose. Barks are graded rather than discrete, and isolation and play barks occupy the same acoustic region, so a recording on its own will never separate them - your situation notes do that work, and if the notes are thin the label is a guess.
  • Your reference set depends on your dog performing each type while you happen to be holding a phone. The rarer entries, compulsive and socially facilitated barking in particular, may never show up, and you cannot learn a category you have never recorded.
  • One dog is a sample of one. Individual signatures are strong enough that dogs can be identified from their bark spectrograms whatever the context, so a catalogue built entirely on your own dog generalises poorly to the next dog you meet.
  • Some entries on the list are symptoms rather than styles. Compulsive barking, and any barking that appears suddenly in an older dog, are clinical questions - the ASPCA's instruction is to have the dog examined by a vet to rule out medical causes before treating barking as a training problem.

Questions people also ask

How many types of dog barks are there?

It depends whether you are counting situations or sounds. The ASPCA's behaviour material names seven situations worth telling apart: territorial, alarm, attention-seeking, greeting, compulsive, socially facilitated and frustration-induced. Acoustically, Yin and McCowan's measurements describe one graded continuum rather than seven distinct call types. The honest answer is seven contexts sitting on a single range that runs from rough and low to clear and high.

Is howling a type of bark?

It is treated separately. The ASPCA gives howling its own guidance, describing it as one of several forms of vocal communication - dogs howl to make contact, to announce their presence and to get attention, and some howl in response to high-pitched sounds such as sirens. Owners meet it in the same situations as barking, which is why Woofpedia files howling as one of its six folders. But if your dog howls when left alone and stops the moment you return, that is a separation question rather than a barking one.

Do different breeds bark differently?

Body size shifts pitch, so a small dog's warning can sound higher than a large dog's greeting - which is exactly why barks should be scored relative to the individual dog. Breed changes the call itself in a few extreme cases: the Basenji is widely described as producing a yodel-like sound instead of a typical bark, usually attributed to the shape of its larynx, though the popular sources for that anatomical explanation are not primary research. What breed does not seem to change much is your ability to read the bark - Pongracz found familiarity with the breed barely affected how accurately people classified recordings.

My dog only barks when the dog next door barks. Which type is that?

Socially facilitated barking, which the ASPCA lists as a category of its own: some dogs bark excessively only when they hear other dogs. It responds better to management than to training - mask the sound, move his resting place to the quiet side of the house, and avoid leaving him alone during the hour the neighbourhood chorus reliably starts.

Does a deep, rough bark mean my dog is aggressive?

It means he is asking for distance, which is not the same thing. Low, rough, unmodulated barking is what dogs produced at a stranger on the doorstep in Yin and McCowan's recordings, and it fits the broader pattern Morton described across birds and mammals, where low broadband sounds go with hostile contexts. Treat it as information handed over in advance. Punishing the warning is how dogs learn to skip it, which leaves you with less notice rather than a calmer dog.
