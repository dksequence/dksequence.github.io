How to clean dust out of a phone speaker

Brush the grille dry with a soft non-metal brush held at an angle, so lint sweeps sideways out of the slots instead of packing in deeper. Lift what came loose with adhesive putty or low-tack tape. Then play a tone that sweeps low to high at full volume for about fifteen seconds. Never poke a pin into the mesh.

Why this happens

The metal slots you can see are not the speaker. Behind them sits a fine mesh, and behind that the driver. Pocket lint packs into the slots and into the weave of that mesh, shrinking the open area that air can move through. That is why a dusty speaker goes quiet and dull rather than silent, and why it degrades in slow steps instead of all at once — every trip in and out of a pocket presses the same lint a little tighter.

There are really two piles of dust and they need opposite treatments. The grey felt sitting in the slots is surface lint: a brush and something sticky remove it, and you can watch it happen. The much finer powder that already worked its way past the mesh and settled on the diaphragm is beyond the reach of any tool you can safely put near a phone. So the order is physical first, vibration last.

A single steady note is a weak tool against dust. A low tone moves the diaphragm a long way slowly; a high tone moves it a short way very quickly. Particles differ in size and in how stubbornly they cling, so sweeping through the whole band gives many more chances to shake one loose than holding one frequency does. That is the real difference between a water tone and a dust sweep — they are not interchangeable.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Take the case off and look into the grille under a bright light

    A fair number of 'muffled' phones turn out to be a case whose speaker cutout has shifted or silted up. Pull the case off, play something, and listen again. Then shine a light into the grille: surface lint shows up as grey felt sitting in the slots. This one look tells you which of the next steps matters. If you can see it, brushing and lifting will do most of the work; if the slots look clean and it still sounds dull, the problem is already past the mesh.

    Time: 1 minute · You need: A bright light, or another phone's torch

  2. Brush it dry, at an angle, with the grille facing down

    Use a soft, non-metallic brush — a clean dry toothbrush, a soft paintbrush, or a camera lens brush. Android Authority's cleaning guide puts it the same way: 'Using a soft-bristled brush, such as a clean toothbrush or paintbrush, gently clean your phone's speaker grilles.' Hold the phone so the grille points at the floor and brush along the slot rather than into it. The angle is the whole trick: pressing straight in packs lint deeper, brushing across walks it to the edge where gravity takes over. Keep everything dry — a damp cloth turns loose dust into paste and leaves its own fibres behind.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: A soft dry brush

  3. Lift the loosened lint with adhesive putty or low-tack tape

    Warm a small piece of adhesive putty between your fingers until it is soft, press it gently onto the grille, then peel it away slowly. Repeat with a clean face of the putty until it stops coming up grey. Painter's or masking tape does the same job. Two cautions: press, do not mash — forcing putty into the holes leaves some of it in there — and skip strong packing tape, which can leave adhesive in the slots that then collects more dust than you removed.

    Time: 3 minutes · You need: Adhesive putty (Blu-Tack type) or painter's tape

  4. Shake out what you cannot reach: play a sweeping tone

    Turn media volume to maximum, unplug earphones and switch Bluetooth off so the sound really comes out of the speaker you are cleaning. Hold the grille facing down and play a tone that rises continuously from a couple of hundred hertz up into the kilohertz range, for about fifteen seconds. Then tap the back of the phone firmly against the heel of your palm a few times, still pointing down. This is the only step that does anything to particles already behind the mesh, because the only thing that can move them is the diaphragm they are sitting on.

    Time: 15 seconds per pass · You need: A sweeping tone and maximum media volume

  5. Re-test against one fixed reference, and stop when it stops improving

    Judge with the same clip every time, and make it speech rather than music — a voice recording exposes dullness far better than a song does. If each round sounds a little brighter, do another. If two rounds in a row sound identical, stop: what is left is packed into the weave of the mesh or is a problem with the driver itself, and neither gets better with more brushing or more volume.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: A short spoken-word recording

Where this approach stops working

  • A brush and putty only ever touch the outer face of the grille. The fine powder that already passed the mesh and settled on the diaphragm cannot be picked out from outside — and because you cannot see it, you also cannot tell whether you are finished or wasting your time.
  • Compressed air usually makes it worse, and both a manufacturer and a testing outlet say so. Apple's cleaning instructions for iPhone state flatly: “Don't use compressed air.” Android Authority's guide gives the reason: “Canned/compressed air may seem tempting, but you could also force dirt further into your phone, or spray some of the can's liquids.” Pressure has no way of knowing which direction you wanted the dust to go.
  • Liquids do not belong near the opening. A damp cloth binds loose dust into a paste that sets inside the mesh, and on alcohol the same guide says 'it's best to avoid using alcohol anywhere near interior components.' That leaves greasy lint — sunscreen, hand cream, sweat-bound fluff — as the case where home cleaning genuinely runs out of road.
  • Most random 'speaker cleaning' clips are a single fixed low note, re-encoded, starting with a few seconds of silence or an intro. A steady low note is the water treatment, not the dust treatment, so plenty of people spend their fifteen seconds playing the wrong thing at the wrong moment.

Questions people also ask

Can I use compressed air on my phone speaker?

Better not. The pressure can drive debris further in instead of out, and canned air can spit propellant liquid into the opening — Android Authority warns you 'could also force dirt further into your phone, or spray some of the can's liquids.' A soft brush plus adhesive putty gets the same lint out with no pressure at all.

Is it safe to clear the holes with a pin or a needle?

No, and it is the one irreversible mistake on this list. Behind the slots is a fine mesh whose entire job is keeping dust and water off the driver. A pin can tear it, a tear does not repair itself, and afterwards the speaker takes in dirt faster than before. Anything you push through also lands exactly where you cannot retrieve it. Android Authority says it plainly: 'Don't use a bent paperclip, sewing needle, pins, or other any sharp object.'

Will alcohol or a damp cloth clean the grille?

Use them on the body if you like, but keep them off the opening. Apple's cleaning guidance for iPhone is a soft, slightly damp cloth that sheds no lint — and in the same breath, “Avoid getting moisture in openings.” Moisture turns loose dust into a paste that sets in the mesh, and alcohol near interior components is a bad trade. Everything that touches the grille itself should be dry.

How often should I do this?

When you notice it, which for a phone that lives in a pocket tends to mean every couple of months. There is no benefit in running cleaning tones daily: extended maximum-volume playback stresses the driver, and dust that is not there yet cannot be removed in advance.

It still sounds dull after brushing and a sweep. What now?

Compare the two outputs if your phone has them — hold it to your ear like a call and listen to the earpiece, then play something through the bottom speaker. If only one is dull, the cause is mechanical: packed mesh or a tired driver, which is a repair-shop question. If both are dull, check that audio is not going out to a Bluetooth device and that no volume limiter or sound preset is switched on.
