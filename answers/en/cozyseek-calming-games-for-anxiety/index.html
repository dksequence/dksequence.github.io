Calming games when you feel anxious

Choose a quiet activity with no clock and no way to lose, such as a jigsaw, a paper seek-and-find book or a slow game of I Spy, and give it 10 to 15 minutes. These are pastimes, not treatment. If anxiety keeps getting in the way of daily life, talk to a health professional.

Why this happens

People searching for calming games usually want one thing: a way to occupy the mind with something gentle for a little while. Slow, low-stakes activities do that well. They give the eyes and hands something to do, they have a small clear goal, and nothing bad happens if you are slow, which is the opposite of the pressure that makes an anxious evening feel worse.

It is important to be clear about what a game is. A game is a pastime. It is not a treatment for anxiety, and this page makes no health claim about any game or app. The US National Institute of Mental Health says that psychotherapy, also called talk therapy, and medication are the two main treatments for anxiety, and that if the symptoms of stress or anxiety will not go away, it may be time to talk to a professional.

What is on offer here is smaller and more ordinary: a few calm things to do with the next fifteen minutes, alongside the self-care steps that health services already suggest, such as relaxation exercises, deep breathing and paying attention to the present moment. If you are in distress, jump to the section on getting help, because that matters more than any activity.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Start with a slow breathing round before you pick anything up

    Sit somewhere comfortable and follow the NHS breathing exercise for stress: breathe in gently and regularly, counting steadily from 1 to 5, then let the breath flow out gently, counting from 1 to 5 again if that helps you. The NHS page says to repeat this for at least five minutes. Keep your shoulders loose, let your hands rest in your lap, and if counting to five feels uncomfortable, use a shorter count that suits you. The goal is not to force calm, it is simply to give yourself a steady rhythm to start from before any activity begins.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: A chair and a clock you can glance at once

  2. Choose one small visual task with no clock

    Pick a single jigsaw, a page of a paper seek-and-find book, or a colouring page. Put the phone in another room or at least face down. Decide on a window of about fifteen minutes but set no alarm, and make a rule that you may stop at any moment without finishing. Begin at one corner or one object rather than scanning everything at once, and let yourself notice colours and shapes. A task that has an obvious next small step, such as the next edge piece, is easier to settle into than a vague idea of relaxing.

    Time: 15 minutes · You need: A jigsaw, seek-and-find book or colouring page

  3. Play a gentle round of I Spy with the five senses

    Look around the room or the picture and name, quietly or on paper, a few things for each sense you can use: three things you can see, two you can hear, one you can feel against your skin. This is not a formal therapy exercise, only a simple way to turn your attention to the present moment, which NIMH describes as the idea behind mindfulness. If a thought drags you away, notice that it did, then return to the next item on the list without any fuss. Take about five minutes, and use a different set of items on another day to keep it interesting.

    Time: 5 to 8 minutes · You need: Pencil and paper, or just your eyes

  4. Move your body for a couple of minutes between activities

    Sitting still with a worry can make it louder. Stand, roll your shoulders back ten times, walk to a window, and drink a glass of water. NIMH lists exercise, regular meals and a sleep routine among its suggestions for coping with stress, so a short walk round the house or the block is in the same spirit. Keep it gentle and brief: two to five minutes is enough to change the scene. Then go back to your jigsaw or picture, or finish if you have had enough.

    Time: 2 to 5 minutes · You need: Comfortable shoes if you go outside

  5. Reach out to a person, then close the session

    End by doing something connected: send a short message to a friend or relative who helps you cope in a positive way, a suggestion that appears in the NIMH stress fact sheet, or simply say out loud that you did something calm for a while. Put the activity away where it is easy to find tomorrow, so that starting again takes no effort. Then notice, without judging, how you feel compared to fifteen minutes ago. If the answer is no different, that is information too, and a reason to try something else or to talk to someone, not a failure on your part.

    Time: 3 minutes · You need: A phone for one message, if you want to

Where this approach stops working

  • None of this treats anxiety. A calm activity can fill fifteen minutes, but it does not address the cause of the worry, and the feeling may return as soon as you stop.
  • Breathing and counting do not suit everybody. Some people find that focusing on their breath makes them more aware of their body in an unpleasant way. If so, skip it and just do the visual task.
  • A paper activity needs setting up. When you are already low on energy, finding a book, clearing a table and starting a jigsaw can feel like too much, so you end up doing nothing.
  • Pictures run out and become predictable. Once you know where everything is in a seek-and-find page, the search turns into recall and the calm effect fades.
  • If anxiety is frequent, intense, or affecting sleep, work or relationships, a pastime is not enough. That is the point at which a professional, not a better game, is the right next step.

Questions people also ask

Do calming games help with anxiety?

This page makes no such claim. Games are a pastime, and some people simply find slow, low-stakes ones pleasant. The NIMH fact sheet names psychotherapy and medication as the two main treatments for anxiety, so if anxiety is a problem for you, a professional is the right place to start.

When should I talk to someone instead of playing a game?

NIMH says that if you are struggling to cope, or the symptoms of your stress or anxiety will not go away, it may be time to talk to a professional. A doctor or other health professional where you live is the right person to ask about support options.

What if I am in crisis right now?

Put the game down. NIMH says that if you or someone you know is struggling or having thoughts of suicide, you can call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org in the US, and in life-threatening situations call 911. Outside the US, contact your local emergency number or crisis service.

How long should I spend on a calming activity?

There is no rule. About ten to fifteen minutes is a comfortable starting point, and the NHS breathing exercise suggests repeating its counted breathing for at least five minutes. Stop whenever it stops being pleasant.
