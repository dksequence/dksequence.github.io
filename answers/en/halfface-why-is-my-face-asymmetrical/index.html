Why is my face asymmetrical?

Faces are asymmetrical because perfect symmetry is rare; research calls small left-right differences ubiquitous. Genetics and everyday pressures such as handedness are cited contributors, and a close camera can exaggerate them. Check with one straight-on photo, and treat a sudden drooping of one side as an emergency.

Why this happens

Asymmetry is the default, not the exception. A 2020 paper in Symmetry, based on 1,246 adult face scans, says "the ideal state of perfect symmetry is rare in nature, and deviations, known as asymmetries, are ubiquitous at both the individual and population level." So the surprise is not that your two sides differ. It is that anyone expects them not to.

The same paper names where a consistent difference between sides may come from. It says directional asymmetry is "considered to be attributable to genetic components or environmental differences between sides (e.g., biomechanical pressures and handedness)." It also describes a second kind, fluctuating asymmetry, as directionally random deviations from perfect symmetry around a zero mean. In plain words: some of it is built in, and some is small random variation.

Photos can add to it. Selfies taken from a close distance typically suffer from perspective distortion, so the parts of your face nearer the lens look larger. A tilted head or light from one side does the rest. If one side of your face looks off in a selfie, check a straight-on, evenly lit picture before drawing any conclusion.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Look at it in a photo, not only the mirror

    A mirror flips left and right, so the face you see there is the reverse of what others see. Start with a photo, because that is the version that matters when you wonder why one side looks different.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: Your phone camera

  2. Rule out the camera first

    Hold the phone at eye level, aim at the middle of your face and keep your head level. Put the phone further back with a timer if you can, since selfies from a close distance typically suffer from perspective distortion. Take 3 shots and keep the straightest. Take several shots rather than one, and compare them, because a slight turn of the head between frames is enough to change how the two sides look.

    Time: 3 minutes · You need: A shelf or helper, and the camera timer

  3. Even out light and expression

    Face a window or lamp so both sides get the same light, and relax your jaw. A smile or a squint changes one side more than the other, and a shadow can look like a difference in shape.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: A window or a lamp in front of you

  4. Look for what changes and what does not

    Compare 2 things on each side: where the eyebrows sit and where the corners of the mouth end. Repeat on another day. A difference that is present in every photo you have ever taken is the pattern researchers describe as usual; a difference that appeared recently is worth asking someone about. Keep notes on which side seems higher or fuller each time, so you can see whether the pattern repeats or only shows up in one lucky or unlucky photo.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: Photos from a few different days

  5. Know the red flags and act on them

    If one side of your face droops and it is hard to smile, the NHS says that is one of the signs of stroke and to call 999. The CDC asks "Does one side of the face droop?" and says to call 9-1-1 right away. Do not photograph it first.

    Time: Immediately · You need: A phone to call emergency services

Where this approach stops working

  • A photo can show that your two sides differ, but it cannot tell you why. Genetics, handedness and other everyday pressures are discussed in research, yet none of that can be read off your own picture.
  • You are comparing by eye. It is hard to say whether a difference is large or small, so many people worry about changes that are well within the usual range.
  • Camera and light keep interfering. Each retake changes the result a little, which makes it hard to tell your face from the shot.
  • Comparing today's photo with last year's is unreliable if the angle, lens and light were different.
  • Nothing in this approach is a medical assessment. Only a clinician can tell a normal difference from a new problem, and the sudden kind needs emergency care, not a photo.

Questions people also ask

Is facial asymmetry normal?

Yes. A 2020 paper in Symmetry says perfect symmetry is rare in nature and that asymmetries are "ubiquitous at both the individual and population level". Small differences between your left and right sides are the usual finding.

What causes one side of the face to differ from the other?

The same paper says directional asymmetry is considered to be attributable to genetic components or environmental differences between sides, with biomechanical pressures and handedness given as examples. It does not say which applies to you, and a photo cannot show that.

Why do I look more lopsided in selfies?

Selfies taken from a close distance typically suffer from perspective distortion, so features nearer the lens are exaggerated. Try a straight-on, evenly lit photo from further away with the timer before you decide.

When should I see a doctor about my face?

Call emergency services at once if one side of your face droops, especially with arm weakness or slurred speech: the NHS says to call 999 and the CDC says to call 9-1-1 right away. For weakness on 1 side of the face, the NHS notes treatment for Bell's palsy is more effective if started within 72 hours, so do not wait.
