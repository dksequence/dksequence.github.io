How to tell what breed your dog is

Start with the paperwork — adoption record, first vet chart, microchip registration, breeder papers. With none of that, describe the dog by structure rather than colour and narrow to a breed group, not a breed. Eyeballing has a ceiling: for 14 of 20 dogs of known ancestry, fewer than half of 923 professionals named any breed the DNA held.

Why this happens

‘What breed is my dog’ is two questions wearing one coat. One is about ancestry, which is a genetics question with a factual answer. The other is about appearance, which is a description. For a registered purebred the two agree and the paperwork settles it in a minute. For a mixed dog they come apart routinely, and how far apart has been measured rather than guessed.

The measurement is uncomfortable. In the 2013 study by Voith and colleagues, 923 people who work with dogs professionally were shown 20 dogs whose DNA ancestry was known. For 14 of the 20 dogs, fewer than half of those professionals named any breed that appeared in the DNA result. For only 7 of the 20 could even half the observers agree on a predominant breed — and in 3 of those 7, the breed the majority agreed on was not in the DNA result at all, as a major or a minor component. Confidence and correctness turned out to be separate things.

The label also explains less than people hope. In 2022 Morrill and colleagues surveyed the owners of 18,385 purebred and mixed-breed dogs and genotyped 2,155 of them, then asked how much of the behavioural variation between individual dogs breed accounts for. The answer was about 9%. Whatever name you land on, it is a weak predictor of the animal in front of you.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Exhaust the paperwork before you guess

    The adoption or rescue record, the first vet chart (vets usually write down a breed impression at the first visit), the microchip registration entry, any breeder or kennel-club papers, and the original listing if the dog was bought online. If the dog is registered with a recognised kennel club, the pedigree is the answer and nothing else is needed. This step costs nothing and it is the only step that can produce documentary evidence instead of an opinion.

    Time: 30 minutes · You need: Your files, plus one call to the vet

  2. Describe structure, not colour

    Write down adult height and weight, head shape and how long the muzzle is compared with the skull, ear set (drop, prick, rose, button), where the tail is set and how the dog carries it, coat type (single or double, wire, curly, smooth), foot shape, and whether the topline is level or sloping. Then search on those. Colour is the trait your eye grabs first and the one that most often sends people to the wrong breed, because unrelated breeds share the same colours and coat patterns while sharing nothing else.

    Time: 20 minutes · You need: A tape measure, a scale, and side-on photos

  3. Narrow to a breed group, not to a breed

    Herding dogs, retrievers and spaniels, scent hounds, sighthounds, terriers, northern spitz types, livestock guardians and mastiffs — each group shares a body plan because each was shaped around a job. ‘A sighthound-shaped dog’ is a claim you can defend from a deep chest, a tucked waist and long legs. ‘A whippet’ is a claim you almost certainly cannot. Stopping at the group is not giving up; it is the level at which a visual answer is still honest.

    Time: 30 minutes · You need: Nothing

  4. Collect disagreement on purpose

    Take three photos — standing side-on, head-on, and a few seconds of video of the dog trotting — and show the same set separately to your vet, a groomer and a trainer. Write down all three answers before anyone hears anyone else's. If they match, your visual read is about as good as visual reads get. If they do not, you have just reproduced the Voith result on a sample of three, and you now know what a single confident answer is worth.

    Time: A week of asking · You need: Three photos and a five-second video

  5. Swab only when the answer has to be right

    A cheek-swab DNA kit is the only method that returns an ancestry breakdown rather than an impression. Embark's own instructions are to keep the dog from eating for 30 minutes beforehand and to swab for 30 to 60 seconds, aiming for the full 60, with results in two to four weeks for its breed tests. Do it when the answer changes something concrete: a landlord or insurer with breed restrictions, a health screening decision, or a suspected breed-linked condition your vet wants to rule in or out. Ask the vet which health findings would actually change your dog's care before you pay for a health panel on top of the breed one.

    Time: 5 minutes of swabbing, 2 to 4 weeks of waiting · You need: A DNA kit and a dog who has not just eaten

Where this approach stops working

  • For a mixed dog, looking is not a reliable method, and that is measured rather than argued. Across 20 dogs shown to 923 dog professionals, fewer than half could name any breed present in the DNA for 14 of them; a majority agreed on a predominant breed for only 7; and in 3 of those 7 the agreed-on breed was absent from the DNA result entirely.
  • The visual route comes with no error bar. It hands you a name and a feeling of certainty and no way to tell a good guess from a bad one. That is how a wrong breed lands on a vet chart at the first appointment and then sits there for a decade, quoted back by every professional who reads the file.
  • A DNA result is only as good as the reference panel it is compared against. Dogs whose ancestry sits outside modern breeds do not resolve into a tidy breed list. Embark, as one published example, tests for more than 50 types of village dog — populations that roam and breed without human direction, which it describes as “not recognized by kennel clubs as a breed” but “genetically identifiable” — and returns a “Supermutt” result when the match to any single breed is too small to assign confidently. Neither of those is a breed name. If your dog comes back that way, that is the answer rather than a failed test, and no amount of looking at the dog will convert it into one.
  • Whatever name comes back, it is a weak predictor of behaviour: about 9% of the behavioural variation between individual dogs, in the 2022 genomics study. If the reason you want the breed is to predict temperament, no method on this page will give you that.

Questions people also ask

Can an app identify my dog's breed from a photo?

A photo tool is doing visual identification, which is the method that came apart in the Voith study when 923 professionals tried it on 20 dogs of known ancestry. It can be a useful way to generate a shortlist. Treat the output as a hypothesis to check against structure and, if it matters, against a DNA test. Woofpedia is not one of these tools — it has no camera and takes no photos.

How long does a dog DNA test take, and how do I swab?

Embark, as one published example, says to keep the dog from eating for 30 minutes before swabbing, to swab for 30 to 60 seconds, and to expect results in two to four weeks for its breed tests. The wait matters for planning: if you need a breed written on a form this week, the test will not arrive in time and you will be back to describing the dog.

My rescue's paperwork says ‘lab mix’. Is that likely to be right?

It is usually a guess made by eye, in seconds, by someone looking at dozens of dogs that week. That is close to the setup the Voith study tested, and in it a majority of professionals agreed on a predominant breed for only 7 of 20 dogs, and were wrong about that breed for 3 of those 7. Read a shelter label as a description of how the dog looked to one person on one day.

Does knowing the breed tell me how my dog will behave?

Barely. The 2022 study of 18,385 surveyed dogs and 2,155 genotyped ones put breed at roughly 9% of the behavioural variation between individual dogs. Breed shifts the odds on a few specific tendencies; it does not describe the animal on your sofa. A month of watching your own dog will outperform the label every time.
