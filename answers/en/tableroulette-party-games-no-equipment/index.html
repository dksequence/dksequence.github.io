Party games that need no equipment

Four games need only people: Two Truths and a Lie for 4 to 20 players, Charades in two teams with a phone timer, Twenty Questions for any size group, and Rock Paper Scissors to settle who starts. Each round takes about five to fifteen minutes, so pick one, agree the rules aloud, and rotate.

Why this happens

The best no-prop party games share three traits: the rules fit in one sentence, everybody plays at the same time or takes very short turns, and nobody is out for long. That is why a handful of old games keep coming back at birthdays, family dinners and first-day icebreakers instead of being replaced by new ones.

The part people rarely plan for is not the game itself but the gaps between rounds: who goes first, who goes next, and what happens to the loser. The games below are the ones with the clearest rules, followed by an honest look at where they get awkward.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Settle who goes first with Rock Paper Scissors

    Two people face each other, count to three and show rock, paper or scissors with one hand at the same moment. If both show the same shape, play again until there is a winner. Encyclopedia references describe it as a fair way to choose between two options and to settle a dispute. With more than two people, the winner of each pair plays the next pair.

    Time: 1 to 2 minutes · You need: Two hands

  2. Two Truths and a Lie

    Each person thinks of three statements about themselves: two true, one false. They say them aloud, the group guesses which one is the lie, and then the speaker reveals it. University extension guides list it for groups of 4 to 20 and for ages 9 and up, and suggest picking unusual but true facts so the lie is harder to spot. Go around the circle once and move on.

    Time: 10 to 15 minutes · You need: Nothing (a scrap of paper helps some players, but is optional)

  3. Charades in two teams

    Split into two teams. One player silently acts out a word or title for their own team while the team guesses; speaking, spelling the word and pointing are normally banned. Use the timer on any phone or kitchen clock to limit each turn, for example one minute. A common convention is to show the number of words with your fingers at the start, and to hold fingers on the inside of the opposite elbow to show which syllable you mean. Agree the topic (movies, animals, jobs) before the first turn.

    Time: 15 to 20 minutes · You need: A timer; prompts invented by the other team

  4. Twenty Questions

    One person secretly picks a thing, person or place. Everyone else takes turns asking questions that can only be answered yes or no. If twenty questions pass without a correct guess, the answerer wins. A good first question is whether it is alive, to narrow the field quickly. The player who guesses correctly becomes the next answerer.

    Time: 3 to 5 minutes per round · You need: Nothing

  5. Close with a harmless penalty rule

    If you want losers to do something, agree it before play and keep it light: tell a short story about your day, hum a famous song until someone guesses it, or pick the next game. Avoid anything that involves danger, mess, money, or embarrassing someone who did not agree to it. Anyone may pass.

    Time: Under a minute per penalty · You need: A group agreement

Where this approach stops working

  • Choosing who goes next is where the arguing happens. Going around the circle is fair but always lets the person on one side start; Rock Paper Scissors only works between two people, so a group of seven needs a bracket and a lot of waiting.
  • Charades and Twenty Questions need a supply of good prompts. The person who has to invent them is out of the fun for a moment, and a group runs out of fresh ideas after a few rounds.
  • Rules differ between households. Whether pointing is allowed, whether a lie in Two Truths can be about the future, how long a charade turn lasts: each of these starts a dispute if nobody said it aloud first.
  • A random choice is only fair if it is actually random. 'I'll pick someone' tends to land on the same quiet or the same loud person every time, and a Rock Paper Scissors player who shows late is hard to catch.
  • Penalties made up on the spot drift. Without a list agreed in advance, the consequence gets harsher the more the group enjoys it, and the shy player is the one who ends up feeling cornered.

Questions people also ask

How many people do you need for no-equipment party games?

Two Truths and a Lie works best with 4 to 20 players according to a university extension guide, and larger groups should split into smaller circles. Charades needs at least two teams, and Twenty Questions works from two players upward.

Which game is best for a group that does not know each other?

Two Truths and a Lie. University extension guides use it as an icebreaker, each person speaks for under a minute, and nobody has to be performative. Charades is better once people are warmed up.

How do you keep a no-prop game fair?

Say the rules aloud before the first turn, decide the turn order by one visible method, and give every player the right to pass. Most fights at a party table are about order and rules, not about the game itself.

What can I use instead of a timer for Charades?

A phone timer is simplest, but a song that lasts about a minute also works: the turn ends when the song does. The point is that guessing stops at a fixed moment and the next team gets a turn.
