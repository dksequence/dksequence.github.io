How to document noise for a complaint

Keep a written noise diary for at least seven days: date, start and stop time, what the noise was, and how it affected you. Be accurate, since councils may use it as evidence. Talk politely to the source first if safe, then send the diary to your local authority. Rules differ by location.

Why this happens

Noise is regulated mostly at the local level. The US EPA explains that in 1981 the Administration concluded that noise issues were best handled at the State and local level, so the rules, limits and complaint process depend on where you live, and often on your council, city or housing provider.

Because of that, there is no single national standard for what counts as too loud. What most local authorities have in common is that they want a record: when the noise happens, how long it lasts and what it does to you. One New South Wales council, for example, says its diary should be completed for a minimum of seven days and that accuracy is extremely important because it may be presented as evidence in court.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Start a noise diary today and keep it for at least a week

    Use a notebook or a note on your phone, but write each entry the same day, while you remember exactly what happened. For every incident record the date, the time the noise started, the time it stopped, and a plain description such as “bass music through the wall” or “dog barking continuously.” Keep the wording factual and avoid guesses about motives or labels for the neighbour. A council in New South Wales, Wingecarribee Shire, asks for a minimum of seven days of entries, so a week is a sensible target for any complaint, and longer shows a pattern better than a single bad night.

    Time: 5 minutes per entry · You need: Notebook or notes file, a clock

  2. Record how it affects you in specific terms

    The effect on you is often the heart of a complaint. Be specific and concrete rather than general. The same council's instructions give examples such as “woke me up” and “prevented studying.” Write what actually happened: woke at 1:40 a.m. and could not get back to sleep, had to leave the living room, could not take a work call. Add where you were when you heard it, such as the bedroom or the garden, because noise that is fine in one room can be unbearable in another. This makes your diary read as a real account instead of a list of grievances.

    Time: 2 minutes per entry · You need: The diary

  3. Add supporting detail that someone else can check

    Note anything that backs up your entries without exaggerating it. Mention if a visitor or another neighbour heard the same noise, and write their name if they agree. Take a dated photo of the source if it is a fixed object such as a vent, a generator or an air conditioning unit. Keep copies of any messages you send or receive about the problem. If your area publishes quiet hours, write down when the noise falls inside them. Do not trespass, film people, or record conversations to build a case, and do not edit or rewrite old entries; accuracy matters more than volume.

    Time: 10 minutes · You need: Phone camera, copies of messages

  4. Make contact politely before escalating

    Where it is safe, tell the person responsible, calmly and in writing if you prefer, what you hear and when, and ask if something can be adjusted. Many problems come from people who simply do not know. Keep a copy of what you send and note the date and reply in your diary. If the noise comes from a rented building, tell the landlord, building manager or housing provider as well, since they often have their own rules. If you feel unsafe, skip this step and go straight to the authority or the police non-emergency line.

    Time: 15 minutes · You need: A short, polite message

  5. Contact the right local authority with your diary

    Search for the noise or environmental health service of your city, council or county. Ask what they accept, since many provide their own diary form and some, like Wingecarribee, ask that it be completed in full and signed. Provide your diary, the dates and your attempts to resolve it. A local officer decides whether the noise breaks local rules. That council says action depends on whether the noise is harmful, outside prescribed times or levels, or interferes unreasonably with the comfort or repose of another person, but that is one place's law, so check yours.

    Time: 30 minutes · You need: Your diary and the local authority's website

Where this approach stops working

  • A diary is your own account. It carries weight because it is detailed, consistent and dated, but nobody else can verify it at the moment the noise occurred, so it can be challenged.
  • Writing it up each day is tedious and easy to abandon, particularly when the noise keeps you awake. Entries made late tend to lose exact times and details.
  • A diary does not tell the officer how loud the noise was. Words like “very loud” mean different things to different people, and the rules in many places depend on levels or on specific hours.
  • Rules, limits and procedures differ between countries, states and councils, and what a local authority accepts as evidence is its own decision. Nothing here is legal advice.
  • Phone-based measurements can add context, but a phone is not a calibrated instrument, and such a reading is not legal evidence.

Questions people also ask

How long should I keep a noise diary?

At least a week. Wingecarribee Shire Council in New South Wales says its diary should be completed for a minimum of seven days. Other authorities may ask for more or less, so check with yours.

Can I use a decibel reading from my phone as evidence?

Treat it as supporting context, not proof. Phone microphones are not calibrated, and an informal reading is not legal evidence. Whether any measurement is accepted is up to your local authority or court.

Who do I complain to about noise?

The US EPA states that noise issues were concluded to be best handled at the State and local level. In practice that means your city or county, council, environmental health service or housing provider. Search for the noise complaint page for your area.

Should I talk to the neighbour first?

If it is safe, a polite conversation or note often fixes the problem, and many authorities like to see that you tried. If you feel unsafe or the situation is already hostile, contact the authority directly.
