How to stop hitting snooze

Set the alarm for the time you really need to be up, not 30 minutes earlier, and put it across the room so you must stand to reach it. Then fix one wake time every day and aim for seven or more hours of sleep. Constant snoozing is often a sign you are short on sleep.

Why this happens

People snooze for a simple reason: the extra minutes feel good. Cleveland Clinic says that if you struggle to get out of bed every morning, you may simply not be getting enough sleep or have an underlying sleep disorder, and that snoozing decreases the REM sleep you get.

The research is not one-sided. A Sleep Foundation report on one study found people performed better on cognitive tests after snoozing and that it limited waking from deep sleep, but that finding concerned night owls, snoozing did not change self-reported mood or sleepiness, and the authors say more research is needed. So the evidence does not say snoozing is dangerous; it says the habit often signals too little sleep, and it can be hard to control.

That is why the steps below focus on the cause and on removing the temptation, not on guilt.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Set the alarm for the time you actually need

    Cleveland Clinic advises setting the alarm for when you actually need to wake up. If you set it 30 minutes early on purpose so you can snooze three times, the broken sleep in between is lighter and you gain little. One alarm, one time.

    Time: 1 minute · You need: Your phone

  2. Move the alarm across the room

    Cleveland Clinic also suggests putting the alarm across the room to reduce the temptation to snooze. Standing up is the hardest part of getting up, and this makes you do it before you can silence the sound. Do the move at night, not in the morning: decide the spot before bed, plug the charger in there, and test that you can hear the alarm from your pillow.

    Time: 1 minute tonight · You need: A charger and a spot out of reach

  3. Keep the same wake time, weekends too

    The Sleep Foundation recommends waking at the same time on weekdays and weekends because a fluctuating schedule keeps you from a consistent sleep rhythm. The CDC says the same: get up at the same time every day. Pick a wake time you could realistically keep on a Saturday, because a schedule that only works on weekdays tends to be dropped within a week or two.

    Time: Ongoing, 1 to 2 weeks to settle · You need: Nothing

  4. Check you are getting enough hours

    The CDC recommends seven or more hours for adults aged 18 to 60. If you go to bed at 1 a.m. for a 6 a.m. alarm, the snooze button is a symptom. Move bedtime earlier and protect it: turn off devices at least 30 minutes before bed and avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening, as the CDC advises.

    Time: Tonight onwards · You need: An earlier bedtime

  5. Plan something for the first five minutes, with light

    Open the curtains, step outside, make a drink. The Sleep Foundation notes that light, especially sunlight, is a key driver of the circadian rhythm. It also says sleep inertia generally lasts 15 to 60 minutes, so expect to feel foggy for a while and get up anyway.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: A window

Where this approach stops working

  • Every one of these steps relies on you, half asleep, obeying a plan you made the night before. On a hard morning the decision often loses.
  • Moving the alarm only helps if you stay up. Plenty of people switch it off and go straight back to bed.
  • Fixing the schedule takes weeks, not a night, and it does not help on the morning after a late one.
  • A fixed wake time is hard with shift work, small children or irregular days.
  • If you cannot get up even after enough sleep, or you are very sleepy through the day, this is not just a habit problem. NHLBI advises telling a healthcare provider if you often feel sleepy during the day or do not wake up feeling refreshed and alert.

Questions people also ask

Is hitting snooze bad for you?

The evidence is mixed. Cleveland Clinic says snoozing decreases the REM sleep you get. A study reported by the Sleep Foundation found better cognitive test results after snoozing in night owls, with no change in self-reported mood or sleepiness, and the authors say more research is needed.

Why do I keep hitting snooze every morning?

Cleveland Clinic says you may simply not be getting enough sleep or have an underlying sleep disorder. Checking your hours against the CDC's seven or more for adults is a good first step.

Should I set my alarm earlier so I can snooze?

Cleveland Clinic advises setting it for when you actually need to wake up.

How do I stop snoozing if I am a night owl?

Gradually move bedtime earlier, keep the same wake time, and get light soon after waking. The Sleep Foundation notes that a fluctuating schedule disrupts your rhythm. If it stays hard, speak with a clinician.

Will an alarm app stop me snoozing?

A snooze limit and missions can make snoozing harder, but they do not fix being short on sleep. Treat the app as support for the schedule steps, not a replacement.
