Your life in weeks

Draw a grid with 90 rows and 52 columns, one box per week, which gives 4,680 boxes. Fill in the boxes for the weeks you have already lived, working from your birth date; at 36 years old that is roughly 1,900 boxes. The picture shows the weeks behind you at a glance.

Why this happens

The idea of drawing a life as a grid of weeks became widely known through Tim Urban's 2014 post on Wait But Why, titled Your Life in Weeks. In it, each row of boxes is one year, and he notes that the grid is large enough to cover a newborn growing into a 90 year old, 4,680 weeks in all. He also writes that the boxes are a reminder that no matter what happens in a given week, you get a fresh box to work with the next one.

The number 90 is a drawing choice, not a prediction. Population averages are much lower: the WHO reports global life expectancy at birth of 73.3 years in 2023, and the US National Center for Health Statistics reports 79.0 years for 2024. Averages describe whole populations and say nothing certain about one person, so treat the 90 rows as paper that is long enough, not a forecast.

The weekly scale works because it sits between two extremes. Days are too many to draw and years are too few to feel. At 52 columns a row is a single year you can recognise at a glance.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Rule the grid: 90 rows by 52 columns

    Take a sheet of paper in landscape or use graph paper, and draw 52 boxes across for the weeks of one year. Repeat down the page for 90 rows, one per year of life, giving 90 × 52 = 4,680 boxes. Draw them small, around 3 to 4 millimetres square, so the whole life fits on a single A3 or a pair of A4 sheets. Number the left edge by age, 0 to 89, and mark every tenth row with a heavier line so you can find a decade quickly. If drawing by hand feels tedious, a spreadsheet works: set the column width to a few characters and fill 52 columns by 90 rows with a light border.

    Time: 20 to 30 minutes · You need: Graph paper or a spreadsheet, a ruler, a fine pen

  2. Work out how many weeks you have already lived

    Subtract your birth date from today's date in days, then divide by 7 and round down. For someone born on 15 March 1990 on 5 October 2026 that is 13,353 days, which is 1,907 full weeks and 4 days left over. Hand counting is the slow route; in a spreadsheet, put your birth date in A1 and today's date in A2 and use =INT((A2-A1)/7). A year is 52.14 weeks, so a row of 52 boxes is a slight simplification: over 36 years the drawn rows fall about 5 boxes behind the real calendar, and that small drift is normal.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: A calculator or spreadsheet

  3. Fill the boxes you have lived

    Starting at the top left, shade boxes left to right, row by row. Each full row is a year of life. For 1,907 weeks, shade 36 full rows (36 × 52 = 1,872 boxes) and then 35 boxes in row 37, which is 1,907 in total. Use a light grey or a pencil so the filled part reads as the weeks behind you without feeling heavy. Anything you leave unshaded is simply blank paper that has not been used yet. You do not need to decide how many rows will eventually be filled.

    Time: 10 minutes · You need: Pencil, marker or the spreadsheet fill colour

  4. Mark the weeks that mattered

    This is where the chart stops being arithmetic. Pick a few colours and mark specific weeks: your birth, school years, moves, jobs, the week you met someone, a trip you still talk about. Put a small dot in the box and add a one word key in the margin. Aim for 10 to 20 marks, no more, so the page stays readable. Seeing a year as 52 boxes tends to show how a single memorable week sits in a row of ordinary ones, and how ordinary weeks make up the great majority of any life.

    Time: 15 minutes · You need: Coloured pencils, a short key

  5. Use it: add one thing for the weeks ahead

    Choose one concrete plan and attach it to the next few boxes. For example, circle the next 4 boxes and write one thing you want in them: a call you keep postponing, a walk with someone, a skill you want to start. Keep the sheet somewhere you pass by, on the fridge or inside a notebook cover. Each Sunday, shade in the week that has just ended. Shading one box takes seconds and is the entire habit; the chart does the rest by showing, plainly, that every week gets used one way or another.

    Time: 2 minutes per week · You need: The finished chart, a pen

Where this approach stops working

  • It is static. A paper chart only changes when you shade the next box, and if you skip a few Sundays it is quietly wrong until you catch up.
  • Drawing and maintaining 4,680 boxes by hand is a one-off project that many people start and leave unfinished, and a printed copy cannot be updated at all.
  • The 52 column grid is an approximation. A year has 52 weeks and one or two extra days, so over a lifetime the grid drifts by a few boxes against the real calendar.
  • The 90 rows are a drawing convention and not a measure of anyone's future. Official life expectancy figures are population averages and cannot be read off a single person's chart.
  • A chart of boxes shows weeks only. It does not turn them into anything you can picture, such as meals, seasons or holidays still ahead of you, and it stays on paper where you will not see it every day.

Questions people also ask

Why 90 years, and how many weeks is that?

Ninety years of 52 weeks each is 4,680 weeks. Tim Urban's 2014 Wait But Why post draws the same 90 row grid, with each row as one year. Ninety is chosen so the paper is long enough, not because it is a prediction. Official averages are lower: the WHO gives 73.3 years globally for 2023, and the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics gives 79.0 for the US in 2024, with 76.5 for males and 81.4 for females.

Does a weekly chart tell me how long I will live?

No. Life expectancy numbers are averages across entire populations and cannot tell any one person their future. The chart shows the weeks you have lived and leaves the rest as blank paper, which is why many people find it motivating rather than final.

How many weeks old am I?

Subtract your birth date from today in days and divide by 7. Someone born on 15 March 1990 had lived 13,353 days, about 1,907 weeks, by 5 October 2026. A spreadsheet formula such as =INT((today-birth)/7) does it in one step.

This chart makes me feel worse, not better. Is that normal?

It can stir up heavy feelings, and it is fine to put it away. If you are having thoughts of ending your life, you do not have to face them alone: in the US you can call or text 988 at any hour, and findahelpline.com lists verified helplines in many countries.
