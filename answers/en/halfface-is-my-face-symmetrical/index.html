Is my face symmetrical?

Almost no face is perfectly symmetrical, and that is normal. To compare yours fairly, take 3 straight-on photos at eye level in even light with a relaxed expression, then judge the straightest one instead of the mirror. Expect small left-right differences; research describes them as ubiquitous.

Why this happens

Perfect symmetry is rare. A 2020 paper in Symmetry, working from 1,246 adult face scans, begins from the point that "the ideal state of perfect symmetry is rare in nature, and deviations, known as asymmetries, are ubiquitous at both the individual and population level." So the real question is rarely whether your face is symmetrical. It is how much, and in which direction.

Researchers separate two kinds. Fluctuating asymmetry is defined as "directionally random deviations from perfect symmetry around a zero mean", while directional asymmetry occurs when the average asymmetry in a population is not zero, meaning one side tends to differ in a consistent way. That second kind is considered to be attributable to genetic components or environmental differences between sides, with handedness and biomechanical pressures given as examples.

What you see is also shaped by how the picture was taken. A photo taken from a close distance typically suffers from perspective distortion, so a selfie can make one side look bigger or flatter than it is. A mirror, meanwhile, flips your face. Before you decide anything about your own symmetry, you need a fair picture to look at.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Use a photo, not the mirror

    A mirror shows you left and right swapped, and you are used to that version of your face. Many people feel a photo of themselves looks slightly "off" for exactly this reason. Take a photo and judge that, because it is what other people see.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: Your phone camera

  2. Shoot straight on, at eye level

    Hold the phone level with your eyes, with the lens aimed at the middle of your face, and keep your head upright without tilting or turning. A tilted chin or a turned head changes how each side looks long before your face does. Take 3 shots and keep the straightest one. Take three or four shots and compare them instead of trusting a single frame, because a slight head turn between shots is enough to change how the two sides look.

    Time: 3 minutes · You need: A phone, or a friend holding one

  3. Keep the camera back from your face

    Selfies from a close distance typically suffer from perspective distortion, as researchers working on selfie correction put it. Part of your face is simply nearer the lens than the rest. Rest the phone on a shelf with the timer on, or have someone shoot from further away, and zoom in afterward.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: A shelf or a helper, plus the camera timer

  4. Use even light and a relaxed face

    Stand facing a window or a lamp so light lands on both sides equally. A light from one side leaves a shadow that looks like a difference in shape. Keep your jaw loose and your mouth in a neutral position, because smiles often pull to one side.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: A window or a lamp in front of you

  5. Compare the halves side by side

    Look at the photo and compare 2 things at a time: the height of your eyes and eyebrows, and the two corners of your mouth. Then flip the photo and look again. Notice small differences without turning them into a verdict. Small left-right differences are what research expects to find. Write down what you notice for each pair before you flip the image, so the second look checks your first impression instead of replacing it.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: The photo and any editing app that can flip an image

Where this approach stops working

  • Your eyes are not a measuring tool. You can see that one eyebrow sits higher, but you cannot tell whether the difference is a few percent or a lot, so judging is mostly a guess.
  • Every retake is slightly different. A small tilt of the head, a different smile or a shift in the light changes the result, so it is hard to know what is your face and what is the shot.
  • Flipping and splicing halves by hand is slow and awkward. Building a full "left-left" and "right-right" face takes an image editor and some patience, and most people give up before they see the result.
  • Close-up lens distortion cannot be removed by looking harder. Moving the camera back helps, but a phone held at arm's length will still stretch the near parts of your face.
  • Nothing here is a medical check. A photo cannot tell you why a difference exists or whether a new change needs attention.

Questions people also ask

Is it normal for my face to be asymmetrical?

Yes. The 2020 Symmetry paper describes asymmetries as "ubiquitous at both the individual and population level" and perfect symmetry as rare in nature. A small difference between your left and right sides is the usual finding, not a defect.

Why does my face look different in a mirror and in photos?

A mirror shows you left and right swapped, and a photo shows what others see. Close-range photos can also add perspective distortion, since selfies taken from a close distance typically suffer from it. That is why you may feel one version looks more like you.

Can a symmetry check tell me anything about my health?

No. A photo check is for curiosity only and cannot diagnose anything. A change that is sudden is different: if one side of your face droops, especially with arm weakness or slurred speech, the NHS says to call 999 and the CDC says to call 9-1-1 right away.

What should I do if one side of my face has suddenly changed?

Do not wait and compare photos. The NHS lists one side of the face drooping as part of the FAST signs of stroke. It also notes Bell's palsy, weakness on 1 side of the face, is treated more effectively if started early, within 72 hours, so contact a clinician promptly.
