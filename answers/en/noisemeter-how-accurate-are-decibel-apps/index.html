How accurate are decibel apps?

It depends on the phone. In its own lab testing, NIOSH found its iPhone sound meter app accurate within about 2 dBA, but it could not verify any Android app. Phone microphones are not calibrated instruments, so treat a reading as a rough estimate, check it against sounds you know, and expect a few decibels of error.

Why this happens

A decibel app turns the phone's microphone signal into a number. Accuracy therefore depends on the microphone, the audio chip and the software between them, and those differ from one phone model to the next. NIOSH, the US government's occupational hearing research body, gives exactly this reason for not vouching for Android apps: the Android market is fragmented among many manufacturers with different microphones, audio chips and software.

The decibel scale is also not intuitive. It is logarithmic, so a few decibels matter more than they look. NIOSH's guidance treats every 3 dBA increase as a reason to halve the allowed exposure time, which means a reading that is off by 6 dB is a four-fold difference in sound energy rather than a small rounding error.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Learn which number you are reading

    Before judging accuracy, know what the number means. Most hearing-safety guidance uses A-weighted decibels, written dBA, which discount very low and very high pitches the way human ears do. A meter showing plain dB with no weighting will read higher on rumbling sounds such as traffic or air conditioning. Also separate the instant value, which jumps around constantly, from the average over time, which is the figure comparable to published limits. Comparing an instant peak to an eight-hour limit is the most common beginner mistake, and it makes any meter look wrong when it is not.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: The meter's settings screen or help text

  2. Check it against sounds with a known loudness

    Use everyday reference points as a coarse sanity check. NIOSH describes 85 dBA as the level at which you must raise your voice to talk to someone 3 feet away, typical of a lawn mower or power tools, and 95 dBA or higher as the level at which you must shout to be heard at that distance. Stand about a metre from a running vacuum cleaner, a blender or a busy street and see whether the reading lands in a believable band. This will not prove accuracy to the decibel, but it quickly exposes a meter that is 15 or 20 dB off, or one that never leaves a narrow range.

    Time: 10 minutes · You need: Two or three steady noise sources you can switch on and off

  3. Compare against a second meter

    If you can borrow a second phone, or a hand-held sound level meter, run both side by side. Put them at the same height, a few centimetres apart, with the microphones pointing the same way, and play the same steady sound. If two different phones agree within roughly 2 to 3 dB across several loudness levels, the reading is probably usable as an estimate. If they disagree by 8 or 10 dB, at least one is badly off, and you should trust neither for anything that matters. Write down the difference at quiet, medium and loud levels, because many cheap microphones are only reasonable in the middle of the range.

    Time: 15 minutes · You need: A second phone or an inexpensive hand-held meter

  4. Measure the way the microphone expects

    A good meter placed badly gives a bad number. Take the phone out of any thick case that covers the microphone opening, keep your finger and palm away from the bottom edge, and hold the phone steady rather than waving it. Stay away from walls and corners, which reflect sound and raise readings. Wind and breath across a phone microphone create low rumbling that reads as loud noise, so shelter it outdoors and do not speak close to it. Hold the phone at the position where you actually hear the noise, at roughly ear height, and keep that position the same each time so the readings can be compared.

    Time: 2 minutes · You need: A steady hand or a small stand

  5. Repeat, average and write down the uncertainty

    One reading means little. Measure the same noise three or four times, at different moments, and look at the average rather than the biggest spike. Then state your result honestly, for example “about 70 dBA, give or take 3 to 5 dB.” When you compare against a limit such as NIOSH's 85 dBA recommended exposure limit for an eight-hour workday, remember that a phone result close to the line is inconclusive in either direction. Only a result far below or far above that line tells you something, and anything that truly depends on the number needs a calibrated instrument.

    Time: 10 minutes · You need: Paper or a notes file

Where this approach stops working

  • Your phone microphone has not been calibrated against a reference, and you cannot tell by looking whether it reads high or low. Even a careful comparison only shows the error at the sounds you tested.
  • NIOSH says lab verification of Android apps is not currently possible because of how varied the hardware is. A good result on one phone says little about another model, and a lab result for one app does not transfer to a different app.
  • Comparing against a second phone only tells you whether two uncertain devices agree. They can share the same bias, so agreement is encouraging but it is not proof.
  • Phone microphones are designed for voices, so very quiet rooms and very loud sources, such as concerts and machinery, are where errors tend to be larger. Treat those readings with the most caution.
  • Doing all this by hand takes a long time, and the result is a note on paper. You will not have a trace of how the level moved over minutes, which is often what you need more than a single number.

Questions people also ask

Are phone decibel meters accurate enough to trust?

They are accurate enough for rough comparisons, such as telling a quiet room from a busy street. For anything that depends on exact numbers, they are not enough. NIOSH tested its iPhone app in a lab and reported accuracy within ±2 dBA, but it states that testing Android apps in its laboratory is not currently possible.

Is an iPhone more accurate than an Android phone?

The only lab evidence we found points one way: NIOSH verified accuracy for an iOS app and could not do so for Android because of hardware variety. That is a statement about what can be tested, not proof that every Android phone is worse or every iPhone is good.

Can I use a decibel app reading as proof of a noise problem or a hearing risk?

Use it as an indication only. Phone readings are not valid for legal or certified work. For hearing safety, NIOSH's recommended limit is 85 dBA averaged over an eight-hour day, so readings far above that deserve attention and readings near it call for a proper measurement.

Why do two apps on the same phone show different numbers?

They may apply different weighting, different averaging times or different built-in adjustments to the same microphone signal. Check whether both show A-weighted values and whether you are comparing an instant value with an average.
