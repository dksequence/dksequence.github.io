How to signal for help when you are lost or stranded

Make three loud, evenly spaced signals, such as three whistle blasts, three mirror flashes or three lights, then pause and repeat. The National Park Service's Hug a Tree guidance says to give three quick whistle tweets, count to 30 and do it again. If you can reach emergency services, call your local emergency number first.

Why this happens

A voice carries a short distance and tires out fast. A whistle makes a sharper, higher sound than shouting, so it is easier for a searcher to pick out, and blowing it costs far less energy than yelling. The National Park Service's outdoor emergency plan puts it plainly: "Blowing a whistle helps people find you and saves your energy from yelling."

The other half of the idea is the pattern. Wind, birds and echoes make random noises all the time, but a group of three on repeat is hard to mistake for nature. Be Ready Utah, a state emergency-preparedness site, states it this way: "Three of anything in a repeating sequence is understood to be an international sign of distress." That one rule works for sound, for light and for fire, which is why it is the thing worth remembering.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Call your local emergency number first if you can

    If you have any phone signal, call the emergency number for the place you are in, because a call reaches searchers directly and the signals below only help once they are close. The National Park Service's outdoor emergency plan tells callers to give GPS coordinates from the phone if available, the trail or area where you started, your last known location, visible landmarks and what you are wearing. If you cannot get a call out, move on to the next steps.

    Time: 2 to 3 minutes · You need: A phone with any signal

  2. Stay put and make yourself findable

    Once you know you are lost, staying in one place is generally the safer choice, because searchers work outward from your planned route and a moving target is harder to find. If you can do it safely, move to an open spot such as a clearing, where sound and light travel farther and you can be seen. Put on bright clothing and keep your pack beside you.

    Time: 5 minutes · You need: Your pack and any bright clothing

  3. Use sound: three blasts, a pause, repeat

    Blow a whistle three times in quick, evenly spaced blasts, then wait and repeat. The National Park Service Junior Ranger "Hug a Tree" page gives the rhythm: "Get out that trusty whistle and give three quick tweets. Then wait. Count to 30. Do it again." Be Ready Utah gives the same signal in words: "Three blasts of a whistle, 'TWEET, TWEET, TWEET', is a universal signal of distress." If you have no whistle, shout in groups of three, but expect to tire quickly.

    Time: Continue in rounds, resting between · You need: A whistle (or your voice)

  4. Use light in daytime: flash a mirror in threes

    Reflected sunlight can be seen from far away. Be Ready Utah says to flash it in repeated series of three. Aim the flash toward a plane, a ridge or a road, and sweep slowly across the spot where you think rescuers might be. Any shiny surface, such as a compact mirror, can work in a pinch.

    Time: A few minutes per attempt · You need: A mirror or any shiny surface, and sun

  5. Use light at night: three flashes or three fires

    After dark, a flashlight or headlamp can send three flashes, then a pause. Be Ready Utah also describes making three fires arranged in a triangle that can be seen from the air, and using chemical light sticks to make a glowing circle. Only light a fire where it is safe and permitted, and never in dry grass or a windy spot.

    Time: Ongoing through the night · You need: A flashlight, headlamp or light sticks

Where this approach stops working

  • A whistle only helps if searchers are within earshot. Sound carries differently over water, through trees and across ridges, so three blasts can go unheard even when someone is fairly close.
  • Your lungs and lips tire. Blowing a whistle again and again in cold, dry air or when you are dehydrated is harder than it sounds, and weak, uneven blasts lose the pattern that makes the signal recognizable.
  • The famous pattern only works if someone nearby knows it. It is widely taught, but not every passer-by will read three blasts as a call for help, so do not rely on it alone.
  • Mirrors need sun and good aim, and light signals need darkness or at least a clear line of sight. None of them help in fog, heavy tree cover or storms.
  • A phone speaker is not a safety device. It is far quieter than a real emergency whistle, it needs battery and a working screen, and it can fail when wet or cold. Carry a real whistle on any trip where you could need one, and treat anything on a phone as a backup, never a replacement for emergency services.

Questions people also ask

How many whistle blasts mean help?

Three. The National Park Service's Hug a Tree page says to give three quick tweets, wait, count to 30 and repeat. Be Ready Utah describes three blasts as a universal signal of distress. Keep the blasts even and the pauses regular.

What if I do not have a whistle?

Use any group of three: three shouts, three mirror flashes in daylight, or three flashlight flashes at night. Be Ready Utah says three of anything in a repeating sequence is understood as a distress sign. A real whistle is still better, so pack one.

Should I keep moving or stay where I am?

Stay in one visible place once you know you are lost, and signal from there. Searchers start from your planned route, and if you keep moving you become harder to find. If your spot is unsafe, move only as far as needed to reach safety.

Can a phone app replace a real emergency whistle?

No. A phone is quieter than a purpose-built whistle and depends on battery, a working screen and weather. Use an app as a backup when you have nothing else, and call your local emergency number whenever you can.
