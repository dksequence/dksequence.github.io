How to increase your push-ups

To increase your push-ups, train 2 to 3 days a week, work in 3 sets of a rep number you can do cleanly, rest 1 to 2 minutes between sets, and add 1 or 2 reps per set once the target feels easy. Slow, steady increases beat daily max attempts, which mostly produce fatigue and sloppy form.

Why this happens

Strength gains come from a small, repeated overload followed by recovery. The ACSM position stand on progression says to raise the load when you can do one to two repetitions more than your current target, and the NHS advice for beginners is to build up slowly and gradually increase the repetitions.

That works the same way for your body weight: change the angle, add a rep, or slow the lowering phase. The trick is knowing exactly where you are today, then changing one thing at a time.

How to fix it without an app

  1. Find your baseline and write it down

    After a short warm-up, do one set of standard push-ups to the point where your form first breaks: hips sagging, head dropping, or elbows flaring out. Count only reps where your chest reaches about a fist from the floor. That count is your baseline. Testing it again every four weeks, not every day, shows real progress without turning every session into an exam.

    Time: 3 minutes · You need: A floor and a notes page

  2. Train on 2 to 3 spaced days a week

    The ACSM progression statement recommends 2 to 3 days a week for novice training, and the CDC and NHS list 2 days a week as the minimum for strengthening. A Monday, Wednesday and Friday pattern gives your muscles and tendons a day in between. Resist the urge to add extra days when you feel motivated; recovery days are when the adaptation actually happens, and they protect your wrists and elbows.

    Time: Plan once, 10 to 15 minutes per session · You need: A calendar

  3. Choose the version where 8 to 12 clean reps are hard

    ACSM recommends that novices use a load matching an 8 to 12 repetition maximum. Translate that to push-ups by changing the angle: hands on a wall or counter if the floor version gives you fewer than 8, knees down as a middle step, and the standard floor version once you pass 12. The NHS example for the wall version is 3 sets of 5 to 10 repetitions. Moving down an angle is not a defeat; it is how you collect enough clean reps to actually improve.

    Time: 2 minutes to choose · You need: A wall, a counter or a sturdy table

  4. Do 3 sets, rest 1 to 2 minutes, and lower slowly

    Do 3 sets of your target, resting 1 to 2 minutes between them; the ACSM gives 1 to 2 minute rests for muscle-building work. Take about two seconds going down and one coming up, since the ACSM guidance includes controlled lowering as part of effective strength work. Stop each set with a rep or two still in reserve. The NHS says to work to the point where you need a short rest before repeating, which is exactly this feeling.

    Time: 10 minutes · You need: A timer

  5. Add 1 or 2 reps per set, then lower the incline

    When you finish all 3 sets easily for two sessions in a row, add 1 or 2 reps to each set. ACSM suggests a small load increase, on the order of 2 to 10 percent, once you can beat your target by one to two reps. When you can complete 3 sets of 12 on the incline, drop to a lower surface and start again around 8. Log every session so that you can see the small steps. If you feel joint pain instead of muscle fatigue, pause and talk to a clinician.

    Time: 1 minute to log · You need: A notebook

Where this approach stops working

  • Progress from 8 to 9 reps is easy to miss when you only judge by feel, so people often think they are stuck when they are not.
  • Counting your own reps while tired leads to rounding up, which makes the log look better than the workout was.
  • A paper log depends on remembering to fill it in, and a streak of consecutive weeks is hard to see in a notebook.
  • Rest timing is easy to skip, so sets blur together and the later ones get worse.
  • No one reminds you to hold a clean depth, so form quietly drifts and the added reps are partly not real.

Questions people also ask

How long does it take to increase push-ups?

There is no sourced timeline, because it depends on your start and consistency. What the sources do support is the method: 2 to 3 days a week, gradual rep increases and spaced recovery. Retest your max about once a month and compare it with your first number.

Should I do push-ups until failure?

Not every set. The NHS says to work to the point where you need a short rest before repeating the activity, which is hard but not form-breaking. Reserve a true max-effort set for your occasional retest.

What if I cannot do a single standard push-up?

Start with the wall or counter version. The NHS lists the wall press-up at 3 sets of 5 to 10 repetitions, and you can move to a lower surface as the reps get easy.

Do more push-ups mean better health?

Not necessarily on their own. A 2019 JAMA Network Open study of 1,104 male firefighters found that more push-up capacity was associated with fewer cardiovascular events, but it was observational and cannot show that push-ups caused it. The CDC still recommends strengthening on 2 or more days a week alongside 150 minutes of moderate activity.
